# Thinking principles

The habits from "Building Judgment" (*The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*, pp. 93–123) that sit underneath the models. Each item gives Naval's point with pages, then how to apply it in a conversation. The "Apply" lines are working guidance added by this skill.

## Judgment and direction

**Naval's point (pp. 94–95).** Wisdom is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions; judgment is wisdom applied to external problems. He rates judgment above raw effort. With leverage, one correct decision can matter more than years of effort, and the direction you choose matters more than how fast you move. You still need the hours to build judgment.

**Apply.** Before optimizing effort, check direction. Ask: "If this works perfectly, is it the thing you want?" Name the long-term consequence of each option, not just the short-term one.

## Clear thinking from basics

**Naval's point (pp. 95–96, 121).** Real understanding rests on foundations you can rebuild yourself. Smart thinkers understand the basics deeply and can rederive concepts from them; heavy jargon is often a sign of shallow understanding. Advanced concepts in a field are less proven than the basics. If you cannot explain something simply, you do not know it. He uses Feynman's *Six Easy Pieces* as the example of building a subject from basics through an unbroken chain of logic.

**Apply.** Ask the user (and yourself) to state the idea in plain words a child could follow. Where the reasoning jumps, rebuild the missing step from basics. Treat fancy terminology as a prompt to check, not as evidence.

## See reality; desire clouds it

**Naval's point (pp. 96–98).** The main thing that blocks us from seeing reality is a fixed idea of how it should be. Desire for an outcome clouds perception. Suffering is often the moment we can no longer deny reality — and so the moment real progress can start. We give friends obvious advice because we do not share their desire; the same distance helps our own decisions. He tries to acknowledge problems openly to co-founders and friends so he cannot hide them from himself. A feeling reports on your guess about the facts; it is not itself a fact.

**Apply.** Ask: "What would you tell a friend in this exact situation?" Separate what the user wants to be true from what the evidence shows. Point out signals the user may be ignoring.

## Make empty time to think

**Naval's point (p. 98).** Without one or two unscheduled days a week, you cannot think, have good ideas, or make good judgments. Great ideas come after boredom, not during busyness.

**Apply.** If the user is rushing a big decision while overloaded, say so. Recommend sleeping on it or blocking time, when the decision allows.

## Shed identity to see reality

**Naval's point (pp. 101–103).** The ego forms in early years and then interprets everything new. Habits are necessary, but we bundle them into an identity and defend them. Take habits apart and ask if they still serve you. Beliefs adopted as part of a group bundle (a party, a religion, a nationality) deserve a fresh check from first principles, and a worldview whose beliefs all sit in tidy bundles is a warning sign. He also says: "To be honest, speak without identity" (p. 102). A contrarian belief that your own tribe rejects is more likely to be real. Personalities, careers, and teams need redesigns too; no setup stays right for long when everything around it keeps changing.

**Apply.** When the user's reasoning sounds like a group position, ask which parts they have checked for themselves. Ask whether an "I am the kind of person who…" statement is a reason or just a habit.

## Independent and contrarian thinking

**Naval's point (pp. 99–100).** Smart people often seem odd because they think everything through for themselves. A real contrarian reasons independently from the ground up and resists pressure to conform; reflexive objection is just conformity of another kind. He calls optimistic contrarians the rarest kind of thinker.

**Apply.** Do not reward disagreement for its own sake. Reward independent reasoning, whether it agrees with the crowd or not.

## Decision habits

**Naval's point (pp. 103–106).**
- The classical virtues are decision heuristics that favor the long term over the short term (p. 103).
- "Self-serving conclusions should have a higher bar" (p. 104).
- Most biases exist because they save thinking time. When a decision is important, drop what you remember and who you think you are, and look only at the problem (p. 104).
- Radical honesty keeps you connected to reality: lying to others leads to lying to yourself. He cites Feynman on not fooling yourself (p. 104).
- Praise specifically, criticize generally — a rule he takes from Warren Buffett — so that egos work for you, not against you (p. 105).
- It is almost always possible to be honest and positive (p. 105).
- Being right a little more often than others creates outsized value when decisions are leveraged; small edges in decision quality compound into nonlinear returns (pp. 105–106).
- "The more you know, the less you diversify" (p. 106).

**Apply.** Raise the bar when the user's conclusion conveniently favors what they already wanted. Remove the identity and history from the question and restate the bare problem.

## Principles over cases

**Naval's point (p. 106).** In decisions the brain acts as a memory-prediction machine. Predicting from "X happened before, so X will happen again" is weak because it depends on specific circumstances. Principles — mental models — generalize better.

**Apply.** When the user argues from a single anecdote or a recent event, ask what principle it supports and whether that principle holds in this case.

## How to keep building models

**Naval's point (pp. 113–123).** The most efficient way to build new models is to read a lot. Build a foundation of high quality: math, hard sciences, and microeconomics; basics before advanced topics; original sources and classics before modern interpretations (for example, Darwin before Dawkins; Adam Smith, von Mises, or Hayek before commentators). Reading in the wrong order lays weak axioms that distort later judgment. For old problems (health, calm, values, family), older solutions that survived long filtering are likely better; for modern problems, read modern material. Teaching what you learned forces learning. For the next sixty days of clearer thinking: read the greats in math, science, and philosophy, ignore news and contemporaries, avoid tribal identity, and put truth above social approval (p. 118). Confusion while reading is like muscle soreness: a sign of growth.

**Apply.** When the user asks how to learn a domain, point to originals and basics first. Use the [reading list](reading-list.md).
