# Models

Naval lists these in the "Collect Mental Models" part of "Building Judgment" (*The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*, pp. 106–113). He frames mental models as compact pointers to principles you already understand; without the underlying experience they are just quotes (pp. 106–107). Use each model to ask sharper questions about the specific case.

Models from outside the Almanack are in [Models from other sources](#models-from-other-sources) at the end.

Every entry has the same shape:

- **Naval's point** — a paraphrase of what the book says, with page numbers. Only this part is attributed to Naval.
- **Use when / Questions / Misleads when** — working guidance added by this skill. It is not Naval's text.

Where Naval says only a sentence or two (evolution, black swans, calculus), the entry says so. Do not inflate his position.

---

## Evolution

**Naval's point (p. 107).** Much of modern society can be explained through evolution; he offers sexual selection as one example of an allocation problem behind human behavior. He groups evolution with thermodynamics, information theory, and complexity as ideas with explanatory and predictive power across many areas of life. This is a brief mention, not a developed framework.

**Use when** explaining persistent human behavior, markets, organizations, or products that survived (or died) through selection.

**Questions**
- What behavior is actually rewarded here, regardless of what people say they want?
- What selection pressure produced this situation? What would survive under the new pressure?
- Is this design built for an environment that no longer exists?

**Misleads when** used to justify behavior ("it is natural, so it is right") or to tell neat just-so stories with no test.

## Inversion

**Naval's point (p. 107).** He does not trust his ability to say what will work, so he eliminates what will not work. Success is mostly about avoiding mistakes and incorrect judgments, not about being brilliantly right.

**Use when** evaluating any plan, idea, or bet — especially when enthusiasm is high.

**Questions**
- How would this certainly fail? Which of those failure paths are we on now?
- What mistakes, if avoided, would make success likely by default?
- What should we stop doing, rather than start?

**Misleads when** it turns into blanket risk-avoidance. Eliminating bad options is a filter, not a substitute for choosing a direction.

## Complexity theory

**Naval's point (p. 108).** Studying complexity showed him the limits of knowledge and of prediction. People are fundamentally ignorant and bad at predicting the future, so he built a way of operating that works despite that ignorance.

**Use when** a plan depends on a forecast, a long chain of causes, or a system with many interacting parts (markets, cities, organizations, ecosystems).

**Questions**
- What in this plan only works if the forecast is right? Can it work without the forecast?
- Where are the feedback loops? What happens second- and third-order?
- How do we keep options open and learn quickly instead of predicting?

**Misleads when** used as an excuse for no plan at all. Uncertainty argues for robust and adaptive plans, not for none.

## Microeconomics and game theory

**Naval's point (p. 108).** Microeconomics and game theory are fundamental; he doubts anyone can succeed in business or navigate modern capitalist society without a strong grasp of supply and demand, labor versus capital, and game theory. Elsewhere he recommends reading a microeconomics textbook start to finish (p. 109) and treats Microeconomics 101 as a solid foundation (p. 120).

**Use when** judging a business idea, pricing, a negotiation, a career move, or any situation with competing players.

**Questions**
- Who supplies this, who demands it, and what happens to price when more people enter?
- Does this reward labor (time) or capital and owned assets?
- Who are the players, what does each one want, and what will they do in response to our move? Is this a one-time game or a repeated one?

**Misleads when** it assumes everyone is a rational, fully informed actor. Real players have limits, emotions, and status goals.

## Principal-agent problem

**Naval's point (pp. 108–109).** He calls this the single most fundamental problem in microeconomics. A principal (owner) cares and does the job well; an agent acting for someone else optimizes for themselves. The smaller the company and the more directly pay is tied to value created, the more people act like principals. He cites the Julius Caesar line about going yourself if you want something done.

**Use when** hiring, delegating, choosing partners, evaluating advisors, structuring compensation, or trusting someone's recommendation.

**Questions**
- Who here is a principal and who is an agent? Whose money and whose reputation are at risk?
- How is each person paid, and what does that pay reward in practice?
- Is the person advising me better off if I say yes, regardless of the outcome for me?

**Misleads when** it becomes general cynicism. Many agents act with integrity; the model points to where to check, not to a verdict.

## Compound interest

**Naval's point (pp. 109–110).** Returns that keep adding onto themselves produce results far beyond intuition — thousands of times the start over decades, not ten or twenty times. Compounding rules in the intellectual domain too, and a small user base growing at a steady monthly rate can surprise even its founders. In his later rules he says all the real benefits in life come from compound interest (p. 224).

**Use when** comparing a short-term gain to a long-term one, choosing what to learn, deciding who to work with, or evaluating growth.

**Questions**
- Does this choice compound (skills, reputation, relationships, owned assets), or does it reset each time?
- What is the growth rate, and what does it imply in five or ten years — not in one?
- What would interrupt the compounding? (A single large loss can wipe out years of gains.)

**Misleads when** it projects a high growth rate indefinitely. Growth curves bend; check what limits them.

## Basic math

**Naval's point (p. 110).** Basic math is underrated. For business and investing you need arithmetic, probability, and statistics — multiplying, dividing, compounding — far more than advanced topics.

**Use when** any claim involves numbers, odds, risk, sizes of markets, or expected value.

**Questions**
- What is the base rate? Out of a hundred similar attempts, how many work?
- What is the expected value, and what is the worst case?
- Does the arithmetic actually add up (units, orders of magnitude, denominators)?

**Misleads when** false precision replaces judgment. Rough numbers with honest ranges beat exact numbers built on guesses.

## Black swans

**Naval's point (p. 110).** He points to the study of tail events — extreme, rare outcomes — and credits Nassim Taleb with pioneering work on it. This is a brief mention; for depth, see Taleb's books in the [reading list](reading-list.md).

**Use when** a plan assumes normal conditions, exposure is large, or a rare event would be catastrophic or hugely positive.

**Questions**
- What rare event would ruin this? Can we survive it?
- Is our downside capped and our upside open, or the reverse?
- Are we treating "it has not happened yet" as "it cannot happen"?

**Misleads when** every remote risk gets equal weight and nothing gets done. Focus on events that are both plausible enough and severe enough to matter.

## Calculus

**Naval's point (p. 110).** Calculus is useful for understanding rates of change and how nature works. The principle — measuring change in small discrete or continuous steps — matters more than solving integrals.

**Use when** progress, growth, or decline is involved, or when the current level hides the trend.

**Questions**
- Is the thing getting better or worse, and how fast? Is the rate itself speeding up or slowing down?
- What small change, repeated, produces the outcome we want?
- Are we judging by the current position when the direction matters more?

**Misleads when** a short trend is extended too far. Rates change.

## Falsifiability

**Naval's point (p. 111).** For a claim to count as science, it must make predictions that could be proven wrong. To believe something is true, it should have predictive power and be falsifiable. He argues macroeconomics has suffered because it rarely makes falsifiable predictions and cannot run controlled experiments. His later rules restate truth as that which has predictive power (p. 224).

**Use when** judging a claim, a study, a guru's advice, a business theory, or the user's own belief.

**Questions**
- What result would prove this wrong? If nothing could, what is it actually claiming?
- Has it made predictions in advance that came true?
- Is this backed by real evidence, or by math and jargon that few people can check? (He notes people overvalue claims dressed in math or pseudoscience because they cannot critique them, p. 119.)

**Misleads when** used to dismiss every claim that has not been formally tested. Many practical decisions must use the best available evidence.

## If you can't decide, the answer is no

**Naval's point (pp. 111–112).** For hard, long-lived choices — marrying someone, taking a job, buying a house, moving city, going into business with someone — if you cannot decide, say no. Modern life offers a huge number of options, and people are not built to feel how many there are. These decisions lock you in for years, so say yes only when you are very confident. If you are building a pro-and-con spreadsheet, that is a sign the answer is no.

**Use when** the choice is long-lived, hard to reverse, and alternatives are plentiful.

**Questions**
- Is this a lock-in decision measured in years?
- Are there many other options of this kind available?
- Am I trying to talk myself into this?

**Misleads when** applied to cheap, reversible experiments or to rare opportunities with no real alternatives. Naval's scope is long-lived choices in a world of abundant options; outside that scope, a quick trial may beat a no. Also check what "no" means: when both options are new lock-ins (job A versus job B, city A versus city B), saying no to one is saying yes to the other. Then treat the indecision as a sign that the decisive factor is missing from the list, and find it.

## Run uphill

**Naval's point (pp. 112–113).** When two choices are about even, take the one more painful in the short term. The mind overweights short-term comfort and avoids short-term pain; if two options are equal and one hurts now, that one likely carries the long-term gain — which compounds. He compares learning from a confusing book to working out a sore muscle.

**Use when** the user is torn between two roughly equal paths and one of them is harder or more uncomfortable now.

**Questions**
- Which option am I avoiding because it is uncomfortable, not because it is worse?
- Which one builds skill, reputation, or health that keeps paying?
- Are the two options actually equal, or is one simply worse?

**Misleads when** pain becomes the goal. The rule is a tiebreaker for options that are otherwise even, not a reason to choose suffering.

## Related judgment rules from the same section

These appear before the model list but work like models. Details are in [Thinking principles](thinking-principles.md).

- **Rederive from basics** (pp. 95–96, 121): if you cannot rebuild a concept from fundamentals, you are only memorizing.
- **Self-serving conclusions need a higher bar** (p. 104): hold conclusions that favor you to stronger proof.
- **Package beliefs are suspect** (p. 102): beliefs that arrive as part of a group identity should be rechecked from first principles.
- **Direction over speed** (p. 95): with leverage, which way you go matters more than how hard you push.
- **Knowledge versus diversification** (p. 106): "The more you know, the less you diversify."

## Models from other sources

These come from outside the Almanack. Each **Origin** line credits the person or field the idea comes from; it is not attributed to Naval. They were collected from a popular "mental models" post (see [Sources](sources.md)), which gives only one-line summaries, so the origins below are checked against the original ideas rather than taken from the post. Where a popular version of the idea overstates the evidence, **Misleads when** says so.

## Parkinson's law

**Origin.** C. Northcote Parkinson, in a 1955 essay in *The Economist*: work expands to fill the time available for its completion. He wrote it as satire about growing bureaucracies.

**Use when** setting deadlines, scoping a project, or wondering why a task with plenty of time still finished late.

**Questions**
- How much time would this take if the deadline were half as long? What would we cut?
- Is the time limit set by the work, or by the calendar slot it was given?
- What is the smallest version that would be "done"?

**Misleads when** used to set deadlines too tight to do the work well. Compressing time cuts scope or quality; decide which on purpose.

## Chesterton's fence

**Origin.** G. K. Chesterton, *The Thing* (1929): do not remove a fence until you know why it was put up.

**Use when** changing, removing, or "simplifying" an existing rule, process, tradition, piece of code, or habit that looks pointless.

**Questions**
- Why was this put here, and by whom? What problem did it solve then?
- Does that problem still exist? What happens the day after it is gone?
- Can we find out cheaply (ask, read the history, try removing it in a reversible way)?

**Misleads when** it becomes a defence of every status quo. The rule asks you to find the reason, not to keep the fence. If the reason is gone or cannot be found after a fair look, removing it is fine.

## Diminishing returns

**Origin.** Classical economics (Turgot, Ricardo): if you keep adding one input while the others stay fixed, each extra unit eventually adds less output. "Too many cooks."

**Use when** deciding whether more of the same (people, hours, money, features, practice of one kind) will still pay off.

**Questions**
- What did the last unit of effort or money actually add?
- Which fixed input is now the bottleneck?
- Would changing the mix beat adding more of the same?

**Misleads when** applied to things with increasing returns, such as network effects, compounding skills, or scale economies. Check which curve you are on before assuming it flattens.

## Pareto principle (80/20)

**Origin.** Vilfredo Pareto observed that about 80% of Italy's land was owned by about 20% of people; Joseph Juran later generalised it as "the vital few and the trivial many". In many situations a small share of causes produces most of the effects.

**Use when** prioritising work, customers, bugs, habits, or spending; or when overwhelmed.

**Questions**
- Which few items produce most of the result here? Do we have data, or a guess?
- What would we stop doing if we only kept the top fifth?
- Is the long tail cheap to ignore, or does it hide a risk?

**Misleads when** 80/20 is treated as a law. The ratio is a rough pattern, not a fixed number; check the real distribution. Some "trivial many" items still matter (safety, compliance, one angry key customer).

## Butterfly effect

**Origin.** Meteorologist Edward Lorenz (1972 talk): in chaotic systems, tiny differences in starting conditions grow into very different outcomes, which limits long-range prediction. Closely related to [Complexity theory](#complexity-theory) above.

**Use when** a plan depends on a long-range forecast, or a small early choice (a default, a first hire, a culture norm) will be copied and amplified.

**Questions**
- Which early, small choices here will be hard to undo once they spread?
- Does our plan survive if the starting conditions are slightly different?
- Where can we check in often instead of predicting far ahead?

**Misleads when** used to claim every small action has huge consequences. Most systems damp small changes; only some amplify them. It argues for humility about prediction, not for paralysis.

## Tacitus razor

**Origin.** Popular-internet heuristic: to find who holds power, look at who you are not allowed to criticise. Despite the name, it is not from Tacitus, and the usual quote is wrongly attributed to Voltaire; its traceable source is a 1990s essay by an American white nationalist writer, and it is often used in conspiracy rhetoric. Treat it with care.

**Use when** asking where real power or protected status sits in a team, company, or community.

**Questions**
- Whose decisions or ideas are never questioned here? Why?
- Is that because they hold power, or because criticising them has a different cost?
- What happens to people who do raise criticism?

**Misleads when** used as proof of hidden control. Some people or groups are shielded from criticism because they are vulnerable, not powerful; and many powerful people are criticised constantly. Use it to raise a question, never as an answer.

## Lindy effect

**Origin.** Named in 1964 after Lindy's deli in New York (Albert Goldman), developed by Benoit Mandelbrot and popularised by Nassim Taleb in *Antifragile*: for non-perishable things like ideas, books, and technologies, the longer something has survived, the longer it is likely to keep surviving.

**Use when** choosing what to read or learn, which tools or technologies to bet on, or judging a fashionable new idea against an old one.

**Questions**
- How long has this been around, and has it survived real tests?
- Am I choosing the new thing because it is better, or because it is new?
- Is this a non-perishable (an idea, a practice) or a perishable (a person, a machine, a company)?

**Misleads when** applied to perishable things, which age and die, or used to reject every new idea. Everything old was once new; Lindy is a prior, not a verdict.

## Dunning–Kruger effect

**Origin.** David Dunning and Justin Kruger (1999): people who scored lowest on tests of a skill most overestimated their own score, and top scorers slightly underestimated theirs.

**Use when** judging your own (or someone's) confidence in a domain they are new to, or weighing advice from a confident non-expert.

**Questions**
- How would I know if I were wrong here? Have I been tested against reality?
- Is this person's confidence backed by a track record in this specific area?
- What do experienced people in this field say is hard about it?

**Misleads when** used as an insult ("you're just Dunning–Kruger"). The popular "Mount Stupid" confidence curve is not from the paper, and part of the measured effect may be a statistical artefact. The useful point is narrow: self-assessment is unreliable when you lack the skill to judge your own work.

## Hawthorne effect

**Origin.** Named after 1920s–30s productivity studies at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works; the term was coined by Henry Landsberger in 1958. People change their behaviour when they know they are being observed.

**Use when** designing experiments, pilots, or metrics; or deliberately using accountability (working alongside someone, public commitments) to change your own behaviour.

**Questions**
- Will the result hold once nobody is watching?
- Does our pilot or trial measure the change, or the attention the change brought?
- Could a little visible accountability help here?

**Misleads when** treated as a strong, settled finding. Later re-analyses of the original Hawthorne data found much weaker effects than the legend. Treat it as a known risk to check for, not a guaranteed boost.

## Halo effect

**Origin.** Psychologist Edward Thorndike (1920): one positive trait (looks, fame, success in one field) colours how people judge someone's other, unrelated traits. The reverse, a "horns effect", also happens.

**Use when** judging a person, company, or product, especially one that is successful, famous, or impressive in one area; or when someone successful gives advice outside their field.

**Questions**
- Which trait is impressing me, and is it relevant to what I am judging?
- Would I accept this claim from someone without that track record?
- What does this person's record show in this specific area?

**Misleads when** it makes you discount real skill that does transfer. Some abilities generalise; check the specific track record rather than dismissing everything.
